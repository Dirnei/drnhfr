## Just because you can build it

In software today you can build almost anything. The tools are good enough
that the how is rarely the problem. And we developers like to build whatever
shines. That is not only our fault: these days a solution tends to count as
better the trendier the buzzword on it. Kubernetes, microservices and event
sourcing are great on their own and solve real problems. But just because you
have a hammer doesn't make every problem a nail. The right tool for the right
problem, and some things don't need fixing at all.

So the real question is not whether you can build something, but whether it is
worth it. A lot of things are nice. Whether they are worth what they cost is
another matter.

The best example of that I have is this site.

<!-- git-stats -->

A business card on the web would have done: who I am, what I do, how to reach
me. One page, one afternoon. Instead there is now a terminal with 32 commands
(and counting). The CV only shows up after a small puzzle in the terminal, the
skills hang in an interactive graph and there is a capture the flag game on top
of that...

Does anyone need that? No. I built it anyway, because I can and nobody has to
pay for it. The site has hardly any real purpose, except that I learned a thing
or two along the way. And of course the most important part: <strong class="shine">IT SHINES!</strong>

As a private decision that was a good one. Had a customer given me a budget for
it, it would have been a pretty bad one, and I would have told them so. I am
not a yes-man, the actual problem matters more to me than the budget. Of course
I want to build cool things too, but where they make a difference, not just
because someone pays for them.

In customer projects this happens all the time, just with someone else's
money. What gets built is what is technically possible or what feels good to
build, and whether it solves the actual problem nobody asks, or only once it is
already standing. That is why I want to know what is really needed before I
start. Often that turns out smaller than expected. Sometimes bigger, but then
at least everyone knows why. Understand first, then build.

The site stays the way it is, though.
