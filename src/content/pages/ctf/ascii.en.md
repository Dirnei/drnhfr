A computer does not store letters, it stores numbers. ASCII decides which
number stands for which character, and has done since 1963. There are 128
characters, the numbers 0 to 127, so each one fits in a byte.

Every row below is one character. On the left is the number in decimal, next
to it the same number in hexadecimal and in binary, and on the right the
character itself. If a puzzle hands you `72 105`, look them up here and you
read `H` and `i`. Hex values are often written with `0x` in front, so `0x41`
is `A`.

The first 32 codes and 127 are control characters. You cannot see them. They
used to drive teleprinters and still do things like a line break (`LF`, 10)
or a tab (`HT`, 9). The table shows their short names instead, and the space
(32) is shown as `SP`.

Upper and lower case letters are exactly 32 apart: `A` is 65, `a` is 97. In
binary that is a single bit, which is why they sit side by side in the wide
view. The digits start at 48, so `0` is 48 and `9` is 57.
